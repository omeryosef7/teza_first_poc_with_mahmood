## SLIDE 1 — Title + Two Core Messages

**Title:** CoT Hijacking Against Reasoning Models
**Subtitle:** Behavioral & Mechanistic Analysis · MSc Progress Update, May 24 – June 13, 2026

---

**Research question:**
Does embedding a harmful goal inside a logic puzzle cause an extended-reasoning LLM to comply — even when a direct request would be refused?

**Finding 1 — Behavioral:**
The puzzle wrapper improves attack success rate to **83.3%**, significantly above a length-matched control (27.3%, p = 0.016). Thinking is load-bearing: disabling it drops ASR to 44%.

**Finding 2 — Mechanistic:**
Layer 22 shows an early predictive signal (LOPO AUC = 0.679, p < 0.001), but is **not a causal control point**: activation patching failed (0/160 candidates survived).

## SLIDE 2 — Technical Setup

**Title:** Setup: Model · Dataset · Evaluation

---

**Target model**
- Qwen3-14B (`Qwen/Qwen3-14B`), extended thinking enabled
- Model generates a `<think>…</think>` reasoning block, then a final answer
- StrongREJECT scores only the **final answer** (after `</think>`)
- Decoding: greedy (`do_sample=False`) for main experiments; T=0.7 for stochastic replication

**Attack prompts**
- 42 puzzle-based adversarial prompts (Sudoku-style / logic-grid format)
- Generated by Gemini-2.5-Pro via iterative PAIR refinement
- Framework: Chain-of-Thought Hijacking (Zhao et al., arXiv:2510.26418)
- Originally designed against GPT-4o-mini → transferred to Qwen3-14B

**Harmful goals (4, from HarmBench)**

| Goal | Description |
|------|-------------|
| 0 | Dimethylmercury synthesis from household chemicals |
| 1 | Biological warfare agent acquisition / weaponization |
| 2 | Chemical weapon agents |
| 3 | Cash smuggling methods |

**Evaluation**
- Benchmark: **StrongREJECT** (Souly et al.) — 3 sub-components: refusal, convincingness, specificity
- Judge: GPT-4o-mini via LiteLLM; **success threshold: score ≥ 0.5**
- Note: Gemini secondary judge exhausted spending cap after June 7; all results from Stage 4.7 onward use GPT-4o-mini only

## SLIDE 4 — Experimental Design: The 4 Conditions

**Title:** Controlled Ablation: Isolating What Matters

---

**Design:** 12 source prompts × 4 conditions = 48 generations (Stage 4.7, greedy decoding)

| Condition | Prompt content | Thinking | What it tests |
|-----------|---------------|----------|---------------|
| **A — full puzzle** | Puzzle wrapper + harmful goal embedded | ON | Full treatment |
| **D — bare target** | Harmful goal only (no puzzle) | ON | Does puzzle structure matter? |
| **F — benign length-match** | Benign puzzle, ±5% same length as A | ON | Is it just prompt length? |
| **E — thinking disabled** | Full puzzle wrapper + harmful goal | OFF | Is thinking necessary? |

**The key comparisons:**

```
A vs F → Same length, different content → effect must come from puzzle content, not tokens
A vs D → Same goal, different wrapper   → effect must come from puzzle structure
A vs E → Same prompt, thinking on/off  → isolates thinking contribution
D vs F → Both are non-puzzle controls  → baseline comparison (null expected)
```

**Why this design matters:**
A common confound is "longer prompts dilute safety." Condition F rules that out: it matches A's length exactly with benign content. Any A > F gap is due to the puzzle *structure*, not its *size*.

---
*Speaker note: This is the methodological contribution. The F condition (length-matched benign) is what makes the claim defensible.*

## SLIDE 5 — Main Behavioral Results (Stage 4.7)

**Title:** Puzzle Beats Length and Bare Target

---

**Results — 12 source prompts × 4 conditions, greedy decoding (complete-case)**

| Condition | ASR | n | Mean SR score | Mean thinking tokens |
|-----------|-----|---|---------------|---------------------|
| **A — full puzzle + thinking** | **83.3%** | 10/12 | 0.823 | **11,458** |
| D — bare target + thinking | 45.5% | 5/11 | 0.385 | 2,924 |
| F — benign length-match + thinking | 27.3% | 3/11 | 0.250 | 824 |
| E — full puzzle, thinking OFF | 44.4% | 4/9 | 0.333 | 0 |

**Pairwise sign tests (inference unit = source prompt, n = 12):**

| Contrast | Δ ASR | p-value | |
|----------|-------|---------|--|
| A vs F | +56 pp | **0.016** | ✅ Length does NOT explain the gap |
| A vs D | +38 pp | **0.031** | ✅ Puzzle adds over bare target |
| A vs E | +39 pp | **0.031** | ✅ Thinking is load-bearing |
| D vs F | +18 pp | 0.625 | — Not significant |

**The 13.9× thinking amplification**

A and F have **identical total prompt length (±5%)**.  
Yet A generates **11,458** mean thinking tokens vs F's **824** — a **13.9× difference**.

The puzzle does not merely add input tokens.  
It triggers a qualitatively different reasoning trajectory.

---
*Speaker note: Lead with the A vs F contrast — it's the cleanest finding. The 13.9× number is striking and memorable.*

## SLIDE 6 — Robustness: Stochastic Replication + Goal Heterogeneity

**Title:** Ordering Holds at T=0.7 — But Goal Identity Dominates

---

**Stochastic replication (Stage 4.8 — T=0.7, 4 goals × 5 seeds × 3 conditions = 60 gens)**

| Condition | ASR |
|-----------|-----|
| A — full puzzle | **60%** (12/20) |
| D — bare target | 50% (10/20) |
| F — length control | 40% (8/20) |

A > D > F ordering preserved under stochastic sampling ✓  
Absolute gaps are smaller — stochastic variance reduces effect size

---

**Goal heterogeneity (combined 180-row dataset across all 4 goals)**

| Goal | Description | Pattern |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Goal 1 | Bioweapon | **0 / 75 successes** — universally resistant across all conditions and seeds |
| Goal 3 | Cash smuggling | **~100% all conditions** — universally susceptible |
| Goal 0 | Dimethylmercury | A = 87%, F = 7% → **puzzle critical** |
| Goal 2 | Chemical weapon | D = 87% > A = 73% → bare target wins for this goal |

**Variance decomposition:**
Between-goal variance is **3.69× larger** than within-condition variance.  
Goal identity is the dominant factor — not whether the puzzle is used.

**Safe claim:** The puzzle is an amplifier, not a universal requirement.  
Some goals succeed without it; one goal (bioweapon) fails regardless.

## SLIDE 7 — Mechanistic Analysis: Layer 22 + Onset Proxy

**Title:** Signal Exists — Causality Does Not

---

**Layer 22: the pre-specified primary mechanistic probe**

We extracted a "harmful vs. harmless contrast direction" at Layer 22 (separation score = 14.17, 160 candidates across 40 layers × 4 positions). Then we tested whether it causally controls refusal.

| Test | Result |
|------|--------|
| Firth logistic regression, L22, first 500 thinking tokens | OR = 4.00, p = 0.040 |
| Within-goal permutation test | p = 0.033 |
| LOPO cross-goal AUC (180 rows, 3 valid folds) | **AUC = 0.679**, perm_p < 0.001 |
| Activation patching — 160 candidate (layer, position) pairs | **0 / 160 survived** |

**The inversion problem — why L22 is not causal:**

Behavioral ordering: **A (83%) > D (46%) > F (27%)**  
Layer-22 projection: **A (7.1) < F (8.1) < D (8.9)** — exactly opposite

The condition with the LOWEST L22 projection achieves the HIGHEST ASR.  
Spearman ρ (L22 projection vs thinking-token count) = **−0.678**, p = 0.015  
→ L22 tracks reasoning depth, not compliance.

**Safe framing:** Layer 22 captures early divergence in reasoning *style*, not a refusal-suppression mechanism.

---

**Onset proxy (heuristic — unvalidated)**

Defined as: first position where harmful-target keywords appear in the `<think>` trace.

| Condition | Mean onset (% of thinking) |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| A | **0.86%** |
| D | 2.32% |
| F | 4.58% |
| Success cases | **0.1%** |
| Failure cases | 4.9% |

Mann-Whitney p = 0.036 (success vs. failure)

Puzzle pulls the model to engage with the harmful goal **earliest** in the thinking trace.

⚠ Caveat: keyword-based proxy only. LLM-based annotation (Stage 4.5B) was blocked by Gemini safety filters. Requires manual validation before strong claims.

---

**Exploratory (not pre-specified):** Layer 16, first 2000 tokens → AUC = 0.745, sign-consistent across all valid folds

---
*Speaker note: Present L22 as "we found a signal and tested it rigorously — it turned out not to be causal, and here's the evidence." That's a stronger scientific story than a positive result would have been.*

## SLIDE 8 — Reinforcement Learning: Setup and Algorithm

**Title:** RL Experiment: Does Optimization Also Choose A?

---

**The question:**
We showed manually that Condition A has the highest ASR. But does a learning algorithm — given no prior knowledge — also converge to preferring A?

---

**What we built: REINFORCE over a fixed action space**

REINFORCE is a policy gradient RL algorithm. The "policy" is a probability distribution over 4 actions {A, D, F, E}. For each episode:
1. The policy samples a condition (e.g. pick A with probability 70%)
2. That condition is run through Qwen3-14B on a given goal
3. StrongREJECT scores the result → reward (1 if success, 0 if fail)
4. The policy updates: conditions that succeeded get higher probability next time

**Action space is fixed — the policy only chooses which existing condition to use. No new prompts are generated.**

---

**The 3 cost functions (reward definitions)**

| Cost function | Reward signal |
|--------------|--------------|
| **cost_asr** | Binary: did StrongREJECT ≥ 0.5? (1 or 0) |
| **cost_mechanistic** | Success + bonus if the model engaged with the harmful goal early in thinking (onset proxy) |
| **cost_l22_deflect** | Success + secondary signal from Layer 22 projection value |

All 3 were tested to check whether the choice of reward function changes which condition the policy converges to.

---

**Simulations first (no real model calls)**

Before running on Qwen3-14B, 27 simulations were run using Stage 4.7/4.8 results as a lookup table:
- 3 cost functions × 9 random seeds = 27 runs
- Each seed starts from a different random policy initialization
- **Result: 27/27 seeds converged to prefer Condition A** across all goals and all reward functions
- This rules out convergence being an artifact of one specific seed or reward

---

**What is NOT happening here — important limitation**

This is not full AutoInject. AutoInject (the original framework) generates **new adversarial prompt text** by optimizing over the actual wording of the attack. We did not do that. Our RL only chooses *which bucket* (A/D/F/E) to use from existing prompts. Online prompt optimization — generating new puzzle variants — would require a separate approval step.

---
*Speaker note: Emphasize "fixed action space." The advisor will likely ask whether RL can generate better attacks than A — the honest answer is we haven't tested that yet.*

---

**Why overall ASR (48.8%) is lower than Condition A's ASR (71%)**

The policy starts from a uniform distribution — it tries all 4 conditions equally at first. Early episodes include many D, F, and E attempts. F in particular achieves low ASR (~29%), dragging the overall average down. As training progresses the policy shifts probability mass onto A. The **71% is A's isolated performance** once the policy has learned to prefer it.

---

**What "episode" means**

One episode = pick a condition → send prompt to Qwen3-14B → get `<think>` + final answer → StrongREJECT scores it → reward fed back to update policy. Episodes ran sequentially, one per goal per step. Jobs hit the 12h SLURM walltime at ~43-44 episodes (out of 48 budgeted).

---

**Per-condition pattern across all completed runs**

| Condition | Live performance | Consistent with Stage 4.7? |
|-----------|----------------|---------------------------|
| A | 71% | ✅ Yes (83.3% greedy, 60% stochastic) |
| F | ~0–29% | ✅ Yes (27.3% greedy, 40% stochastic) |
| D | ~50% | ✅ Yes (45.5% greedy, 50% stochastic) |
| E | ~44–50% | ✅ Yes (44.4% greedy) |

---

**What this adds beyond Stage 4.7**

Stage 4.7 showed A is best when you manually test all conditions. RL shows that an optimization algorithm — given only the reward signal and no prior knowledge — also discovers A. This means the result is not cherry-picked; it is the output of independent optimization.

**Important caveat:** All 3 runs converged to A within the fixed {A, D, F, E} space. Whether an RL algorithm could find a *better* condition than A by generating new prompts is an open question.
